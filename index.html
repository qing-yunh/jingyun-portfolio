Case 04 · Merchant Cash Advance · Embedded Finance

Merchant Cash Advance — working capital from POS data

SME merchants generate continuous POS transaction data, yet remain invisible to traditional credit models. At Zhongxin Finance, Haike Rongtong's lending subsidiary, I built a POS-based merchant cash advance — revenue-based repayment, alternative-data underwriting, powered by the group's own acquiring network — turning that data into pre-approved working capital drawn down inside the merchant workflow.

Bottom lineTransaction data makes fast underwriting possible — but a transaction-only view can't see a merchant's other debts, which is exactly why it has to cap how much you're willing to lend.

01 Problem 02 Solution 03 Key decisions 04 Impact 05 Where this transfers
01

Problem

SME merchants struggle to access working capital. Approvals are slow and complex, lenders lean heavily on traditional credit history, and "data-invisible" businesses get turned down at a high rate.

The root cause is that traditional lending models run on outdated financial documents, so they can't see how a business is actually performing right now. Yet these same merchants generate POS transaction data every day — a fresher, richer credit signal than any annual statement could offer.

That gap isn't evenly distributed. A merchant with three years of filed accounts and a banking relationship gets served. A two-year-old restaurant with strong daily takings and no filing history usually doesn't, even though it's often the better credit. In effect, the lending decision was being made on paperwork availability rather than on how the business was actually performing.

Two constraints shaped everything that followed. The loans were small and short, so the cost of assessing one had to be close to zero — a manual credit file was never going to work. And because these merchants sat on our own acquiring network, we could see their takings but almost nothing else: no balance sheet, no reliable declared revenue, no visibility into what they already owed elsewhere.

02

Solution

A POS-based embedded lending product, built on the Haike Rongtong group's own acquiring network (the same payment rails I worked on in Case 05) and integrated directly into merchant workflows:

  • Data-driven scoring — transaction revenue and stability feed real-time credit assessment, replacing traditional credit files.
  • Embedded experience — pre-approved offers surface inside the POS dashboard; merchants apply with one click instead of filling out bank paperwork.
  • Automated repayment — repayments deduct proportionally from daily transaction flow, matching repayment to cash flow.
  • Risk control — real-time monitoring plus a hybrid decision engine combining automation with manual review.

The scoring engine also drew on alternative data, such as social reputation signals and local economic data, to sanity-check risk. The whole thing was packaged as a white-label lending solution for partner banks too, cutting their time-to-market and scaling distribution through a partner ecosystem. The lending itself was bank-funded, so it had to meet the institutional standards partner banks required end to end, from the credit product through to the origination process.

03

Key decisions

Decision 1 — Underwrite on takings, not on filings

The alternative was to use bureau data where it existed and decline where it didn't. That would have been cheaper to build, but it would have rejected exactly the merchants the product was meant for.

So we built the score on what we could actually see: transaction volume, revenue stability across weeks, seasonality, and the behavioural signals around how a merchant ran their terminal. Contextual sources like local economic data and reputation signals were layered on top to sanity-check the picture, not to drive the decision.

The catch is that a transaction-only view is blind to leverage. We could see what a merchant earned but not what they already owed elsewhere. That's a real limitation of the model, and it set a ceiling on how large a facility we were willing to write.

Decision 2 — Repay from flow, not on a date

A fixed monthly instalment is simpler to build, explain and collect. It's also the wrong shape for a business whose takings halve in a quiet month.

We deducted a proportion of daily transaction flow instead: good weeks repay faster, quiet weeks repay slower, and the merchant is never asked for money the business didn't make that day. Because we sat on the acquiring rails, collection happened in the settlement path rather than by chasing a direct debit — the same position that gave us the credit signal gave us the repayment mechanism too.

What it costs the merchant is certainty: they lose a fixed sense of when the facility ends, and the product gets harder to explain in one sentence. That's a real cost, and it lands on the disclosure design rather than on the credit model.

Decision 3 — Hybrid decisioning, and where to put the line

Full automation was fast but wrong at the edges. Full manual review was defensible but far too expensive for facilities this size. We chose a hybrid: auto-approve clear passes, auto-reject clear fails, and route the ambiguous middle to a human.

The harder question wasn't whether to have a manual tier, but how wide to make it. Too narrow and the model's errors go straight through. Too wide and the operations cost eats the margin on every loan. We sized the referral band against reviewer capacity rather than a target approval rate, and revisited it as the model's confidence improved.

Decision 4 — Package it for banks rather than only lending ourselves

The group could originate on its own acquiring base, but that base was finite. Partner banks had capital and licence coverage we didn't, and we had merchant data and a workflow position they didn't.

Packaging the scoring and origination flow as a white-label product traded margin per loan for reach. It also imposed a useful discipline: a bank partner won't accept a model it can't interrogate, so the decision logic had to be explainable, not just accurate. That requirement ended up improving the product for our own book as well.

05

Where this transfers

The UK version of this problem is already being built. Revenue-based finance now sits inside merchant platforms rather than beside them: YouLend inside marketplace and PSP checkouts, Liberis behind acquirer relationships, iwoca’s OpenLending API inside accounting and banking tools. The distribution insight is identical — the lender that reaches the merchant inside a tool they already open every day wins on cost of acquisition before it wins on price.

The mechanics differ in two ways that matter. UK underwriting draws on Open Banking cash-flow data and Companies House structure rather than a proprietary POS network, which means the data is richer but not exclusive, so the moat has to come from the decision model rather than from owning the rails. And whether Consumer Duty applies depends on the borrower’s legal form, a distinction I worked through in Case 02.

What carries over is the harder part: designing repayment that flexes with takings rather than fixing a monthly amount against a seasonal business, deciding where in the merchant’s workflow the credit decision belongs, and holding the line between Risk’s exposure ceiling and Sales’ approval-rate target. That last tension isn’t a data problem, and no amount of alternative data resolves it. It gets resolved by finding the segment where the disagreement actually changes the answer.

Case 05 · Payment Infrastructure · Licensed Acquiring

Aggregated Payment Platform — one terminal, every payment rail

At Haike Rongtong, a licensed Chinese payment institution, I owned an aggregated payment platform built around proprietary POS terminals — unifying banks and digital wallets into a single in-store ecosystem supporting QR-code and card-present transactions, with automated D+1 settlement (most funds land within hours in practice) underneath.

Bottom lineRouting on value rather than cost, and settling every rail through one ledger, is what let the platform add payment methods without adding reconciliation debt.

01 Problem 02 Solution 03 Key decisions 04 Impact 05 Where this transfers
01

Problem

Small offline merchants in China juggled a separate collection code and account for every wallet (WeChat Pay, Alipay, UnionPay), with reconciliation scattered across each provider's own app, no unified view of takings, and slow access to settled funds.

Bank-card acceptance lived on separate terminals from wallet QR codes, doubling hardware and operational overhead. Failed transactions and timeouts at the till directly hurt in-store conversion. And as a regulated acquiring business, every merchant had to pass KYC/AML onboarding — which, done badly, kills conversion at the very first step.

02

Solution

A full acquiring product line for small merchants, built on unified aggregation infrastructure:

  • One QR code, every wallet — a single static code plate at the till accepts WeChat Pay, Alipay and UnionPay QuickPass (C-scan-B); POS terminals and scanning devices handle merchant-initiated B-scan-C, with the QR resolver detecting the payment type and routing to the correct rail.
  • One terminal, card and QR together — proprietary POS terminals integrating banks and digital wallets, so card-present and QR transactions share one device, one workflow and one set of records.
  • Payment reliability engineering — timeout handling, retry mechanisms and smart routing across channels by success rate, cost and latency; transaction analytics and A/B testing to cut failed payments and speed up checkout.
  • Settlement & funds access — automated D+1 (calendar) / T+1 (working day) settlement to the merchant's bound bank account, though most payouts land within hours in practice; a clearing engine and double-entry ledger keep every movement accurate and auditable.
  • Merchant onboarding at scale — agent-network-driven merchant acquisition with KYC/AML workflows built into the intake flow, balancing regulatory rigour with onboarding conversion.

This platform sits within the Haike Rongtong payment ecosystem, where I worked across the acquiring rails, the lending products built on them (Case 04), and the restaurant SaaS that productised the full stack (Case 03). The acquiring platform later evolved into Maqian (码钱), Haike Rongtong's lightweight acquiring product, adding capabilities such as encrypted mini-program remote payment links and cloud-speaker payment voice broadcast.

03

Key decisions

One terminal for two acceptance worlds

Bank cards and wallet QR payments had entirely different flows, devices and settlement paths. We unified them on proprietary POS terminals instead — one device, one merchant workflow, one set of transaction records — rather than bolting a QR reader onto a card terminal. That choice halved merchant hardware overhead and made unified reconciliation possible.

Routing strategy — value-aware channel selection

Routing purely on cost is the obvious first move, and the wrong one. A failed £200 payment at a till doesn't cost you the fee. It costs you the sale, and often the merchant's confidence in the terminal.

So we routed on value instead: high-value payments went to the highest-success-rate channel, low-value payments to the cheapest channel that still cleared reliably. Success rate stayed the primary factor, with cost and latency behind it — which meant the routing table had to be maintained against live channel performance, not set once against a rate card.

The downside is that this makes the merchant's effective cost of acceptance vary with basket size, and that's harder to explain in a pricing conversation than a single blended rate.

Ledger first — financial accuracy as a product feature

The cheap path is to reconcile each provider's export separately and let finance operations absorb the difference. It works fine with two providers and collapses at four, because every new rail adds another format, another settlement cadence and another set of edge cases nobody owns.

We standardised transaction records across all rails into a double-entry ledger instead. Adding a channel became an integration task rather than a finance-operations project, and disputes could be answered from one authoritative record instead of cross-referencing three exports.

This mattered beyond tidiness. For a licensed institution, the ledger isn't internal plumbing — it's what you produce when a regulator, an auditor or a merchant asks what actually happened to a specific payment, and that answer has to be the same every time it's asked.

04

Impact

+15%Transaction completion rates across QR-code and card-present channels
−20%Checkout drop-offs — optimised payment flows, timeout handling and retry mechanisms

05

Where this transfers

In-store acceptance in the UK consolidated around cards and contactless rather than QR, so the surface looks different. Dojo, SumUp and Zettle compete on terminal economics, settlement speed and onboarding time rather than on wallet fragmentation. But the underlying product problems are the same ones I worked on: routing for success rate rather than cost alone, timeout and retry behaviour at a till where the customer is physically waiting, settlement speed as an acquisition lever, and reconciliation that has to stand up to audit.

The closest structural analogue to what QR did in China is Pay by Bank on Open Banking rails, a cheaper alternative rail trying to win share from cards. It faces exactly the problem the QR rails faced early on: a lower merchant cost isn't enough on its own, because the person who has to change behaviour is the customer at the counter, and they don't see the interchange. Whoever solves the checkout experience decides whether the cheaper rail ever gets volume.

The design principle I'd carry directly is the ledger. Standardising transaction records across providers in a double-entry ledger, rather than reconciling per provider, is what made adding a rail an integration task instead of a finance-operations project. It's a decision worth making before you need it, because it's very expensive to retrofit once three providers are live.

Case 03 · SaaS × FinTech Ecosystem

Restaurant SaaS — operations build the habit, finance builds the business

Built for the Haike Rongtong payment group: an all-in-one SaaS ecosystem for the restaurant industry, integrating POS, ordering, inventory and CRM with core financial services — turning restaurant operational data into a real-time credit engine.

01 Problem 02 Solution & strategy 03 Key decisions 04 Impact 05 Where this transfers
01

Problem

Restaurants ran on disconnected systems: POS and ordering, inventory, kitchen display, CRM, payments and settlement, all separate from each other. The result was low efficiency, no unified view of the data, and financing that was hard to get to.

The real insight was that restaurants aren't unqualified for credit — they're data-invisible. The operational data already exists; it just never gets used for financing. Which pointed to a simple vision: turn restaurant data into a real-time credit engine.

02

Solution & strategy

A four-step platform strategy, executed in sequence:

  • Step 1 · SaaS adoption — build daily-use operational tools (POS, inventory, KDS, CRM) to establish habit and system reliance.
  • Step 2 · Payments integration — embed native payment processing to capture continuous transaction flow and initial payment revenue.
  • Step 3 · Embedded lending — launch one-click credit products, using captured transaction data to keep the application step light.
  • Step 4 · Financial ecosystem — deploy automated accounting and treasury, expanding into a holistic financial suite.

The strategic logic is straightforward: control the transaction flow and the financial services follow. Operational software produces data flow, data flow makes underwriting possible, and embedded lending turns that into recurring revenue — each stage earning the right to the next as the product moves from a SaaS tool towards a financial platform.

03

Key decisions

Embed, don't bolt on

A standalone lending product means a separate app, a separate application process, and a merchant who has to decide, unprompted, that today is the day to seek credit. Most never do — not because they don't need the money, but because the moment they need it is usually the moment they're least able to spend an afternoon on paperwork.

Embedding the offer in the POS dashboard changed who initiated. The merchant didn't go looking for finance; finance turned up where they were already checking last night's takings, priced against numbers they recognised as their own.

That only works if the operational software is genuinely used every day, though. A restaurant that opens the dashboard twice a week never sees the offer, and produces a credit signal too thin to lend against — which is why the SaaS product had to be good on its own terms first, and why pulling the finance product forward would have broken both.

North Star: revenue per merchant

Merchant count is the flattering metric, and the misleading one. A platform can add restaurants indefinitely while the ones already on it drift towards dormancy, with every headline number improving right up until churn arrives.

We used revenue per merchant as the North Star instead, supported by four pillars: acquisition (onboarding rate), engagement (daily active merchants), revenue (loan volume, payment GMV) and risk (default rate, fraud rate). The pillars are deliberately in tension: approving more loans lifts revenue and worsens risk, loosening onboarding lifts acquisition and depresses engagement, so no single team could optimise its own number without the trade-off becoming visible.

In practice, this turned engagement into a credit input rather than a marketing metric. A merchant whose daily usage was falling was a merchant whose credit signal was degrading — which meant the growth team and the risk team were reading the same number for different reasons, and had to agree on what it meant.

04

Impact

1,000+Merchants onboarded, with increased financial product usage across the base
+30%Daily active usage — stronger day-to-day merchant engagement
£1.4M+Annual revenue, from subscription and financial-services income beyond transaction fees

05

Where this transfers

Vertical SaaS moving into financial services is a well-established pattern in the UK and US: Lightspeed and Square both lend against transaction data their own tills generate. The sequencing question is the one I had to answer in practice — operational software has to earn genuine daily use before the data is good enough to underwrite on. A restaurant that opens your POS twice a week produces a credit signal you can't lend against, so the finance product is gated on the boring software working first.

That ordering has a commercial consequence people underestimate: it means the SaaS business has to be viable on its own for eighteen months before the financial product contributes anything. The temptation to pull the lending forward, before engagement is deep enough, is exactly how these platforms end up with a thin book and a bad loss rate.

The UK difference is the regulatory gate. Lending to a limited-company restaurant sits outside CONC; lending to a sole trader may not, which changes affordability testing and disclosure design rather than just the paperwork. I worked that distinction through in Case 02. The practical call is to build to the stricter standard and treat the lighter one as a subset, rather than branching the product and maintaining two journeys.

01 Problem 02 Solution 03 Key decisions 04 Impact 05 What I took forward
01

Problem

A services business at this scale lives or dies on two things: whether it qualifies to bid, and whether it can deliver predictably once it wins. Both were constrained.

Larger government tenders required CMMI Level 3 certification the group did not yet hold. Internally, delivery standards varied across a very large project-management population, and resourcing, reporting and process data lived in disconnected systems, so nobody had a reliable view of delivery health across the portfolio.

02

Solution

CMMI Level 3. I was brought in on the strength of having led a certification programme before, and ran this one end to end — process definition, evidence, appraisal readiness and organisation-wide rollout. Certification opened access to a materially larger tender pool.

PMO governance. Standardised delivery methodology, performance management and operating rhythm across a PMO of 100+ project managers — consistent stage gates, reporting and escalation rather than per-project improvisation.

Group ERP, built 0→1. I owned product architecture, module design and process design for the firm’s internal ERP, re-engineering delivery, resourcing and reporting processes across an organisation of more than 10,000 people. This was a product problem, not a procurement one: the workflows had to fit how the business actually delivered.

Client delivery. Alongside the internal programme I worked on systems transformation for government and state-owned clients across banking, audit and environmental agencies — from pre-sales and bid work through to implementation.

03

Key decisions

Decision 1 — Build the ERP rather than buy one. Off-the-shelf ERP assumes a manufacturing or finance shape. A project-services firm is organised around billable people, utilisation and delivery stages. Configuring a packaged product to that shape would have cost more than building one, and it would have locked the process model to the vendor’s assumptions.

Decision 2 — Standardise the process before automating it. Automating an inconsistent process just makes the inconsistency faster. CMMI came first deliberately: it forced a defined, repeatable delivery process, which then became the specification the ERP encoded.

Decision 3 — Govern by exception, not by inspection. With 100+ project managers, reviewing everything is not an option. The reporting model surfaced deviation from stage gates rather than status for its own sake, so PMO attention went where delivery was actually at risk.

05

What I took forward

This is where the compliance-heavy, multi-stakeholder side of product work became second nature: delivering into regulated government environments where the process is part of the deliverable, not overhead bolted onto it. When I moved into payments in 2014, that turned out to be the transferable part — financial infrastructure has the same shape.

The specific frameworks differ in the UK: FCA authorisation, SM&CR accountability and Consumer Duty outcome testing, rather than CMMI and tender qualification. But the product consequence is identical. Evidence has to exist before anyone asks for it, decisions need an audit trail at the moment they're made rather than reconstructed later, and “we will document it after launch” is not a plan. Teams that treat this as a compliance tax ship slower than teams that build it into the delivery process, because the tax arrives anyway, and it arrives late.

The ERP work left a second habit: when the same data is being re-entered in three places, that is a product problem rather than a discipline problem. It is the same instinct that later made a unified transaction ledger the first thing I wanted in a payments platform.

Where this transfers

The call at the centre of this project was whether to build one authoritative data layer with reusable services, or let every department build its own. That's the same call that shows up in fintech, just with different nouns. Who this is for: a working scenario

Amara, 34, is an NHS nurse in Manchester, originally from Lagos. On the 25th of every month, she sends £280 home to her mother — rent, plus whatever is left for her younger brother's school fees. She's switched apps three times in two years, each time after a transfer arrived short of what she expected, or later than promised. She now re-verifies the quoted amount against a separate FX converter before trusting the app's number — leaving the app to check the app is the clearest signal checkout has failed at its actual job.

Amara is the implicit user behind every pain point and metric below: someone making a small, recurring, high-stakes transfer, for whom a £4 discrepancy or a half-day delay is a real constraint on a real month, not a rounding error.

Her corridor isn't a marginal case, either. The UK–Nigeria remittance corridor has historically carried some of the highest costs in the World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide dataset: 8–10% total cost via traditional channels, against a global average of 6.36% and a UK-wide average near 5.7%. Digital-first providers have brought this down to roughly 3–5%, which is exactly the gap that makes checkout trust decisive — Amara has more reason than most to distrust a quoted rate, because the market she's transacting in has a real history of obscuring it.

Practitioner's Note

This persona isn't generic market research. It's drawn from a pattern I saw directly while designing merchant cash advance (MCA) underwriting within the Haike Rongtong payment group: small, recurring transactions from financially cautious users are far more sensitive to broken trust than to absolute price. A merchant shorted once on a daily repayment calculation would dispute every subsequent one, even when correct. The fix here is the same shape: show the full calculation before commitment, not after.

01

Market Context

The UK cross-border remittance market is valued at roughly USD 24bn, inside a global market of USD 208–238bn growing ~7% CAGR. Margins are thin and shrinking — competition has compressed take rates below 50bps in corridors like UK–India, while G20 targets call for retail costs to fall to 1% by 2027.

  • Regulatory pressure is documented, not theoretical — in May 2025 the FCA published Consumer Duty findings on international payment pricing: transaction fees were "not always clearly displayed," intermediary bank fees were "often not displayed up front," and cost information sometimes required extra scrolling to find. This is regulator-verified evidence for the fee-opacity pain point in Section 4.
  • Fintech-led disintermediation — Wise reported active customers up 21% to 15.5M and cross-border volume up 22% to ~£145bn, growth attributed to rate transparency over new rails.
  • Near-zero switching cost — UK users hold multiple financial apps; first-transaction trust at checkout is the decisive retention moment.
Regulatory framing used throughout this case study: the FCA's four markers of "good practice" — recipient amount shown up front, fees itemised and totalled, variable-fee scenarios disclosed, and cost info surfaced without extra navigation — are treated as functional requirements in Section 5, not aspirational design goals.

04

Pain Points

The first and fourth are drawn directly from the FCA's May 2025 Consumer Duty findings — not assumptions. Each is restated in terms of what it does to Amara's monthly £280 transfer.

  • Fee opacity — the FCA's own review found fees were "not always clearly displayed." For Amara, sending into an 8–10%-baseline corridor, that gap is the £15–20 that decides whether her brother's school fee is fully covered.

Stakeholder Tensions & Trade-offs

None of the five interventions were uncontested. Naming where they'd meet resistance — and what I'd give up to ship anyway — is the difference between a wishlist and a roadmap decision.

Tension 1: Treasury vs. locked-rate quote

Holding a rate for 48hrs means open FX exposure at scale. My call: shorter lock window for card-funded transfers (which mostly complete fast anyway); reserve the full window for bank-transfer-funded transfers. Trade-off: a less generous guarantee for some users, in exchange for a hedging exposure Treasury can approve in week one.

Tension 2: Compliance vs. progressive KYC

Tiering by amount creates a new, auditable threshold, and wrong-footing it risks regulatory action. My call: don't fight this one. It runs as a parallel workstream, not a blocking release. This is the biggest-impact pain point, and also the one I won't promise a ship date for without Compliance's sign-off.

Tension 3: Growth/Design vs. the trust strip

Every added element is a hypothesis it won't cost conversion. My call: keep it, but A/B test it specifically against first-time vs. repeat senders — trust deficit is highest for first-timers and may be redundant for repeats. Show it conditionally if the data says so.

Practitioner's Note

This three-way tension between Treasury wanting less exposure, Compliance wanting less audit risk, and Growth wanting less clutter is a normal operating condition for a payments PM. At Haike Rongtong, the same triangle existed around POS lending limits: Risk wanted lower caps, Sales wanted higher caps. The resolution was almost never "pick a side." It was finding the segment where the disagreement actually mattered.

Who this is for: a working scenario

Priya, 41, owns a two-site Indian restaurant business in Leeds: one long-running site and a second opened 18 months ago. Her busiest trading weeks are also when she has to pre-order stock and pay suppliers ahead of revenue landing. Practitioner's note

This persona is built on direct experience, not just market research. Within the Haike Rongtong payment group I worked on embedded lending for restaurant and retail merchants — credit lines repaid as a percentage of daily revenue, underwritten substantially from the merchant's own transaction history rather than filed accounts. The core insight transfers directly: a business's bank or POS transaction data is a faster, fairer signal of repayment capacity than a stack of PDFs and a credit score built for a different kind of borrower.

01

Market Context

Traditional banks' share of new UK SME lending fell to roughly 41% in 2023, down from a clear majority a decade earlier — handing volume to fintech lenders willing to underwrite faster and on different evidence.

  • Speed is the axis of competition — iwoca lent £730m across 35,000 loans in 2024 (up 76% YoY); its CRIF/Credit Passport integration delivers instant decisions up to £25k and 24-hour decisions up to £500k, built on Open Banking-sourced data.
  • Embedded finance has normalised lending-at-point-of-need — iwoca's OpenLending API embeds business credit into Tide, Xero and FundingOptions; a growing share of borrowers never visit a standalone lender at all.
  • The opportunity isn't Open Banking itself, which is now table stakes since the 2018 mandate — it's what a lender does with the data once consent is granted, and how transparently it explains the resulting decision.
A regulatory nuance most product teams get wrong

SME lending is generally an unregulated credit activity — Consumer Duty does not automatically apply just because the borrower is a small business. The actual trigger is the borrower's legal structure: a loan to a limited company falls outside the Regulated Activities Order definition entirely, but a loan to a sole trader or small partnership can fall within CONC — meaning Consumer Duty does apply.

The practical call: build affordability and disclosure to the higher bar (as if Consumer Duty applied) rather than relying on the narrow technical exemption — both because the FCA has written to SME lenders asking them to apply the spirit of Principles 6 & 7, and because it is simply the right way to lend.

Why Now — a dated, structural gap

  • The gap is structural, not cosmetic — iwoca's speed is bundled with a revolving facility at 26–49% APR; Funding Circle's low cost is bundled with a 2-year eligibility bar. Neither offers a fast, Open Banking-decisioned, fixed-term loan at a cost between the two for a business with 6–24 months of trading. A new entrant doesn't need to out-build iwoca — it needs to deliberately serve the segment both are structurally not optimised for.
  • A regulatory tailwind with a deadline — UK late-payment reform (statutory terms tightening to 60, then 45 days; 8% statutory interest above base rate) is live policy. During the transition window, SMEs face inconsistent payment timing — precisely when affordability-checked, fast working capital is most valuable.
  • Investable, not just useful — a £15k, 12-month loan at ~29% APR generates ~£2,150 gross income; the product only works if the affordability engine keeps bad debt comfortably below that. That's exactly why the rules engine is treated as a governance-gated long pole (Section 07), not a fast-follow feature.

Problem & Pain Points

  • Static, backward-looking risk data — the second site's 18-month youth looks riskier on a bureau file than its actual, visible cash flow supports.
  • Decision opacity — "does not meet our lending criteria" gives a declined SME no actionable next step, damaging trust and raising support volume.
  • Affordability blind spots that cut both ways — under-assessing risks arrears and mis-selling; over-indexing on caution declines genuinely creditworthy businesses like Priya's.
  • Regulatory ambiguity drives one-size-fits-all design — because legal structure determines whether Consumer Duty applies, teams that don't model it explicitly default to treating every applicant identically, missing the actual compliance picture.

Stakeholder Tensions & Trade-offs

None of these features were uncontested. Naming where they'd meet resistance — and what trade-off was made — is the difference between a wishlist and a roadmap decision.

Tension 1 · Risk vs Growth

Risk wants conservative affordability thresholds; Growth wants to avoid declining good young businesses. My call: keep thresholds conservative for limited companies under 12 months trading, but allow a wider band for 12–24 months of consistently positive cash flow — trading a little risk for materially better access to creditworthy young businesses.

How we'd know it's wrong: if early arrears for the 12–24 month cohort rise materially above the under-12 cohort within two quarters, narrow the band's eligibility — don't abandon segmentation.

Tension 2 · Build vs Buy on Open Banking

Engineering prefers building against bank APIs directly; Risk/Compliance notes that means holding the firm's own FCA AISP authorisation. My call: buy via Codat (distributing TrueLayer's access, removing the AISP requirement) for MVP and the first 12 months; revisit once volume justifies the fixed cost of direct integration.

How we'd know it's wrong: if per-call cost exceeds an in-house AISP build within 18 months, or connection reliability drops below the success-rate metric, accelerate the revisit.

Tension 3 · Compliance vs Speed on disclosure

Compliance wants full CONC-style disclosure for everyone; Product notes most applicants are limited companies for whom that's confusing friction. My call: add an explicit legal-structure detection step (Companies House lookup) and branch disclosure accordingly — neither over-disclosing to all nor under-disclosing to the regulated minority.

How we'd know it's wrong: a self-declared sole-trader rate diverging from UK business-population benchmarks signals misreporting — add a verification cross-check rather than abandon branching.

Reflection

This exercise is a deliberate translation of prior experience across payment routing, merchant risk scoring and embedded POS lending into UK Open Banking and responsible-lending language. The underlying judgment is the same one that shaped embedded MCA underwriting (Case 04): using a business's own transaction behaviour as the primary signal of repayment capacity, rather than defaulting to static documentation. Only the regulatory framework, the data source and the borrower have changed.
